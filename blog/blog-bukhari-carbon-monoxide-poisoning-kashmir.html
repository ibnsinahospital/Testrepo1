The Silent Winter Killer: Carbon Monoxide from Bukharis — What Every Kashmiri Family Should Know

Every winter, Kashmir loses lives to a gas nobody can see, smell, or taste. It comes from the Bukhari burning in the corner of the room. Here is how carbon monoxide poisoning happens, how to spot it in your own home, and how to keep your family safe through Chilai Kalan.

📅 27 September 2026 ⏱ 13 min read Ibn Sina Hospital

A Bukhari is more than a heater in Kashmir. It is the heart of the room. It is what you gather around on a freezing evening, what dries wet clothes, what warms your hands after a long walk through snow, what makes the winter bearable. For generations, no Kashmiri home has survived Chilai Kalan without one.

But here is something every Kashmiri household needs to hear.

Every winter, families across the valley go to sleep beside a burning Bukhari and never wake up. The cause is carbon monoxide (CO) — a gas you cannot see, smell, or taste. It is the leading cause of accidental poisoning deaths in Kashmir during winter, and it kills silently, usually at night, usually in a room that felt perfectly warm and safe.

At Ibn Sina Hospital in Ompora, Budgam, our emergency team treats families from across the valley every winter — severe headaches, vomiting, confusion, unconsciousness. Many of them had no idea anything was wrong until someone in the family did not wake up.

This is not about giving up your Bukhari. It is about understanding how it can turn deadly — so you can keep your family warm and alive.

Why Kashmir's Winter Makes This So Dangerous

Several factors combine to make carbon monoxide poisoning unusually common in the Kashmir Valley:

  • Chilai Kalan — the 40 days of severe cold from late December through January, when heating demand peaks
  • Sealed living spaces — windows taped, doors blocked, every gap closed to conserve heat
  • Traditional heating — wood- and coal-fired Bukharis, and unvented LPG heaters, burning in small rooms
  • Overnight burning — the stove kept alight all night while the whole family sleeps
  • Blocked or unserviced chimneys — exhaust pipes that are cracked, rusted, or never cleaned
  • No CO alarms — almost unheard of in Kashmiri homes

The good news: every single one of these is a modifiable risk factor. Small changes can make a big difference.

What Is a Bukhari — and Why Does It Turn Deadly?

A Bukhari is the traditional metal heating stove used across Kashmir. It burns:

  • Wood — most common in rural households
  • Coal — common in Srinagar and towns
  • Kerosene — less common, but extremely dangerous
  • Unvented LPG heaters — carry the same risk in small rooms

The problem is not the stove itself. It is the combination of:

  1. Incomplete combustion — fuel burning with too little oxygen
  2. A sealed room — no fresh air entering, no gases escaping
  3. A blocked or faulty chimney — exhaust going nowhere
  4. Burning overnight — hour after hour while everyone sleeps

When fuel burns with enough oxygen, it produces carbon dioxide and water vapour — relatively harmless. When fuel burns with too little oxygen, it produces carbon monoxide instead. In a sealed room during Chilai Kalan, that switch happens fast.

Why Carbon Monoxide Is Called the Silent Killer

Carbon monoxide gives you no warning at all:

  • No colour — you cannot see it
  • No smell — you cannot detect it by nose
  • No taste — you cannot taste it in the air
  • No irritation — no coughing, no burning eyes, nothing

Most people who die of CO poisoning simply fall asleep and never wake up. There is no struggle, no alarm, no chance to escape.

The room does not smell of smoke. The fire looks normal. Everything feels fine — until it is too late.

How Carbon Monoxide Harms Your Body — Step by Step

The scientific mechanism is well understood. Here's how it works:

Step 1: The Stove Consumes the Oxygen

In a sealed room, a burning Bukhari uses up oxygen faster than ventilation can replace it. The air becomes oxygen-poor, and combustion becomes incomplete — producing carbon monoxide instead of carbon dioxide.

Step 2: CO Enters Your Bloodstream

You breathe in the CO without noticing anything. It passes straight from your lungs into your blood.

Step 3: CO Steals Oxygen's Place in Your Blood

Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin — the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen — roughly 200 times more strongly than oxygen does. It forms a compound called carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb). Your blood literally stops carrying oxygen, even though you are breathing normally.

This is why the symptoms come on so suddenly and so silently.

Step 4: Every Organ Begins to Suffocate

Without oxygen, the brain and heart are affected first. This is systemic tissue hypoxia — oxygen starvation of the entire body. It progresses from headache to confusion to unconsciousness to cardiac arrhythmia to death.

Step 5: The Cumulative Effect of a Whole Winter

A single evening in a poorly ventilated room may only cause a headache. But night after night, for weeks of Chilai Kalan, the exposure adds up — and the night that kills is often not the first one.

What the Evidence Shows

Medical research and hospital data from Kashmir and other cold-climate regions have documented:

  • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin with an affinity roughly 200 times greater than oxygen, causing rapid systemic hypoxia even at low concentrations
  • Winter emergency admissions for domestic heating toxicity rise sharply every year across J&K hospitals during Chilai Kalan
  • Death most often occurs during sleep — victims never wake to feel the warning symptoms
  • Fatalities cluster in sealed rooms with burning wood or coal stoves, and in rooms using unvented LPG heaters
  • Survivors of significant exposure can develop delayed neurological damage — memory loss, personality change, tremors, poor concentration — appearing hours or days later
  • Biomass and coal stoves also release high levels of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), worsening asthma, COPD, and heart disease, especially in women and children

The pattern is clear: it's not bad luck. It's ventilation.

Warning Signs You Shouldn't Ignore

Early carbon monoxide poisoning looks exactly like a cold, flu, or food poisoning. That is what makes it so dangerous.

Mild Poisoning

  • Dull, persistent headache — usually the first sign
  • Dizziness or light-headedness
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Unusual tiredness and sleepiness
  • Blurred vision
  • Feeling foggy or "not quite right"

Moderate Poisoning

  • Severe, throbbing headache
  • Confusion and difficulty thinking clearly
  • Intense drowsiness — a strong urge to sleep
  • Chest pain or tightness
  • Rapid heartbeat (palpitations)
  • Shortness of breath, even at rest
  • Weakness in the legs

Severe Poisoning — Medical Emergency

  • Loss of consciousness or fainting
  • Seizures
  • Difficulty breathing or stopped breathing
  • Irregular heartbeat
  • Cherry-red or pinkish skin (a late and unreliable sign)
  • Coma
  • Death during sleep

Two clues should make you suspect carbon monoxide immediately:

  • Symptoms improve when you leave the room and return when you go back in
  • More than one person in the same room develops headache, nausea, or dizziness at the same time — flu and food poisoning rarely affect an entire room together

If you notice this pattern, get everyone into fresh air immediately and go to hospital.

What To Do in a Carbon Monoxide Emergency

Act fast. Every minute counts.

1. Protect Yourself First

Do not rush blindly into a closed room full of CO — you may become a victim too. Hold your breath, open doors and windows from the outside if possible, and get the person out quickly.

2. Move Everyone to Fresh Air

Get the affected person — and everyone else in the room — outside or to a well-ventilated area immediately. Do not wait to see if they feel better first.

3. Switch Off the Source

If it is safe to do so, put out the Bukhari, close the fuel supply, and open all windows and doors.

4. Call for Help

Call for an ambulance or arrange immediate transport to the nearest hospital. At Ibn Sina Hospital, Budgam, our emergency team is available 24/7 — call 9622552553 or 9419023501.

5. Check Breathing

If the person is unconscious, check for breathing. If breathing has stopped and you are trained, begin CPR immediately and continue until help arrives.

6. Keep Them Warm — But Never Over Direct Heat

Wrap the person in blankets. Do not place them directly over a Bukhari, Kangri, or open flame. Direct heat can cause severe tissue damage, especially if there is also cold injury.

7. Go to Hospital Even If They Feel Better

Carbon monoxide can cause delayed neurological damage hours or days later. Anyone with moderate or severe exposure needs medical evaluation, oxygen therapy, and observation — even if they seem fully recovered.

How to Reduce Your Risk Without Giving Up Your Bukhari

You don't have to stop using a Bukhari. But you can make changes that dramatically lower your risk.

1. Clean the Chimney Every Winter

Before the first lighting of the season, have the chimney and exhaust pipe cleaned and inspected. Soot build-up, bird nests, and debris block the escape route for dangerous gases.

2. Check the Exhaust Pipe for Cracks and Leaks

Rusted, cracked, or loosely joined pipes leak carbon monoxide straight into the room. Replace damaged sections and seal every joint properly.

3. Always Keep a Ventilation Gap

This is the single most important rule. A burning stove needs a continuous supply of fresh air. Leave a window slightly open, or keep a vent unblocked, even in extreme cold. A fully sealed room with a burning stove is never safe.

4. Never Sleep With the Bukhari Burning in a Sealed Room

Put the fire out before you sleep, or before the room is closed up for the night. Most carbon monoxide deaths in Kashmir happen during sleep.

5. Burn Dry Fuel, and Watch the Flame

Damp wood and poor-quality coal smoulder instead of burning cleanly, producing far more carbon monoxide. A dull, smoky, slow fire is a warning sign. Also avoid using unvented LPG heaters in closed bedrooms.

6. Install a Carbon Monoxide Alarm

If you can afford one, place a CO alarm near sleeping areas — not directly next to the stove. It is one of the most effective protections available, and it costs far less than a hospital admission.

7. Never Use Direct Heat on Cold-Injured Skin

Do not hold frozen hands or feet over Bukhari coals or a Kangri to rewarm them. Rapid direct heat causes tissue damage and can turn a treatable cold injury into gangrene.

When to Get Emergency Care

You should go to hospital immediately if you or anyone in your home has:

  • Headache, dizziness, or nausea that improves when you leave the heated room
  • Confusion, unusual drowsiness, or difficulty concentrating
  • Chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath
  • Vomiting after sleeping in a heated room
  • Loss of consciousness, even briefly
  • A pregnant woman, infant, or elderly person exposed to stove fumes
  • Anyone found unconscious near a Bukhari — even if they woke up

Evaluation may include:

  • Clinical and neurological examination — to assess brain function
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb) level — measured by blood gas analysis
  • ECG — to check for heart rhythm problems
  • Pulse oximetry and blood gas analysis — to assess oxygen levels
  • High-flow oxygen therapy — the primary treatment
  • Observation — for delayed neurological symptoms

Frequently Asked Questions

What is carbon monoxide poisoning from a Bukhari?

It is a medical emergency caused by breathing carbon monoxide — an invisible, odourless gas produced when wood, coal, or kerosene burns with insufficient oxygen. In a sealed Kashmiri room during winter, CO can reach dangerous levels within hours.

Can a Bukhari really kill someone overnight?

Yes. If the room is sealed and the stove keeps burning overnight, carbon monoxide can build up to lethal levels while the family sleeps. Victims typically lose consciousness and die without ever waking.

What are the first signs of carbon monoxide poisoning?

A dull headache is usually the earliest sign, followed by dizziness, nausea, fatigue, and confusion. If symptoms improve when you step outside and return when you go back in, suspect carbon monoxide immediately.

Is it safe to sleep with a Bukhari burning?

No. Never sleep in a sealed room with a burning Bukhari, coal stove, or unvented LPG heater. Put the fire out before sleeping, or make sure the room has continuous ventilation.

How much ventilation is enough?

There is no single safe number, but the rule is simple: a burning stove always needs a continuous supply of fresh air. Leave a window slightly open, or keep a vent unblocked, even in extreme cold. A fully sealed room with a burning stove is never safe.

Does opening a window really make a difference?

Yes, significantly. Even a small opening lets fresh oxygen in and lets carbon monoxide escape. It also prevents the room from becoming oxygen-depleted — which is exactly what causes incomplete combustion in the first place.

Can I use an LPG heater in a closed bedroom?

No. Unvented LPG heaters produce carbon monoxide and consume oxygen. They should never be used in a sealed bedroom overnight.

Are children more at risk?

Yes. Children breathe faster, have smaller blood volumes, and their bodies are more sensitive to oxygen deprivation. Infants and young children are often the first to show symptoms.

What should I do if I suspect carbon monoxide in my home?

Open all windows and doors immediately, put out the stove, move everyone to fresh air, and go to the nearest hospital. Do not wait to see if symptoms pass on their own.

Do I need to go to hospital if I feel better after getting fresh air?

Yes, if you had moderate or severe symptoms — confusion, drowsiness, chest pain, or loss of consciousness. Carbon monoxide can cause delayed neurological damage that appears hours or days later. Medical evaluation and oxygen therapy are essential.

What is carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb)?

It is the compound formed when carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin in your blood. Because CO binds about 200 times more strongly than oxygen, it blocks oxygen transport and causes oxygen starvation throughout the body.

Can I buy a carbon monoxide alarm in Kashmir?

CO alarms are increasingly available online and in major electronics stores. If you can afford one, place it near sleeping areas. It is one of the most effective protections available.

Does the Bukhari cause other health problems besides CO poisoning?

Yes. Burning wood and coal indoors releases fine particulate matter (PM2.5), which worsens asthma, COPD, and heart disease — especially in women and children who spend long hours indoors.

Is a headache after sleeping near the Bukhari normal?

No. A morning headache after sleeping in a heated room is not normal — it is a warning sign of carbon monoxide exposure. Never dismiss it.

Can carbon monoxide poisoning be treated?

Yes. The primary treatment is high-flow oxygen, sometimes in a hyperbaric chamber for severe cases. Early treatment dramatically improves outcomes. Delayed treatment increases the risk of permanent neurological damage.
